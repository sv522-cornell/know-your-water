Common questions

Plain answers, no water background needed.

Is it safe to drink water from my tap in Ithaca?

For most people, yes. All three public water systems reported no violations in their latest reports. The two things to keep an eye on are old pipes inside your own home and private wells, which nobody tests for you.

Water 101

Do I need a water filter?

Not usually on public water. If your home was built before 1986, or a baby or pregnant person lives with you, a filter certified to for lead is a simple extra step. Look for that label on the box, and change the cartridge on time.

Why does my water smell like a swimming pool?

That's the disinfectant your water plant adds to keep germs out on the way to you. It's usually not a health risk. A pitcher in the fridge makes the smell fade.

Is hard water bad for me?

No. Hard water has extra calcium and magnesium. It leaves white spots and scale, but it isn't a health risk.

What do "ppb" and "ppt" mean?

They're tiny amounts. One part per billion (ppb) is about one drop in a backyard swimming pool. One part per trillion (ppt) is a thousand times smaller than that.

While you wait

Use cold water for drinking and cooking, and run the tap if it sat overnight. That lowers lead from old pipes no matter who owns them.

If your landlord doesn't answer

You can test your own tap; a lead test is the one most worth doing in an older building. Keep your emails and any results. If your building has its own water supply that meets either test above, it's a regulated system and county health can follow up: 607-274-6688.

Small building with its own well? In New York, a water supply is a regulated public water system if it has at least 5 service connections, or regularly serves an average of at least 25 people a day for at least 60 days a year. A small building that meets neither test, like a two-unit house on its own well with one connection, isn't regulated, so nobody is required to test its water or send tenants a yearly report. That's a good reason to ask, or to test yourself.

Check your pipe with a key and a magnet

Find the pipe where water comes into your basement or utility room, before the meter. Gently scratch it with a key, then hold a fridge magnet to it.

LeadDull gray and soft. A key scratches it easily, to shiny silver. A magnet won't stick. Often has a rounded bulb near the shut-off valve.
Galvanized steelDull gray. Hard to scratch, and the scratch stays dull. A magnet sticks.
CopperThe color of a penny. A magnet won't stick.
PlasticWhite, black or blue rigid pipe. A magnet won't stick.
  • Check the pipe where it comes through the wall, near the meter or main shut-off valve.
  • Scratch gently with a key or coin. Never use a knife.
  • Lead and galvanized both look gray. The magnet decides.

Guide based on the EPA's Protect Your Tap lead pipe check.

Why the white spots on my dishes?

Ithaca's water is hard: it carries minerals like calcium and magnesium. They leave spots and scale, but they're not a health risk.

Fewer spots and less scale

  • Set your dishwasher's hardness setting to the number above.
  • Use rinse aid in the dishwasher.
  • Soak faucet aerators and showerheads in vinegar to clear scale.

Thinking about a softener?

  • It's a comfort choice, not a safety one.
  • Salt-based softeners add sodium. On a low-sodium diet, ask your doctor, or keep one unsoftened tap for drinking.

How much PFAS is in Ithaca's water?

Very little. In 2024 federal testing, the City of Ithaca and Cornell found none of the two main PFAS chemicals, and Bolton Point found trace amounts well under the federal limit.

Federal limit for public water systems: 4 each for PFOA and PFOS

Seen scarier numbers?

Some sites compare results to their own health guidelines, which are stricter than the legal limit, or grade your zip code by nearby factories. KETOS's free PFAS grade lookup works that way; in its own words, it's a risk assessment, not a water test. Both are real yardsticks. The chart here uses the legal limit and what was actually measured.

On a private well

Algae bloom on the lake. Is my tap water OK?

If you're on public water, yes. State health officials say public water is treated, disinfected and monitored, and people on it don't need to take special steps during a bloom. The risk is at the lake and the shoreline.

What Bolton Point does

Bolton Point draws from deep in Cayuga Lake, about 400 feet offshore. It watches the lake at its intake every day and has a treatment plan ready. No bloom has ever reached its intake. It doesn't publish algal toxin results for treated water.

At the lake

  • Know it: bright green, paint-like water or scum on the surface
  • Avoid it: stay out, keep pets out, rinse off after contact
  • When in doubt, stay out

Never drink untreated lake water

Boiling doesn't remove algal toxins, and neither do home filters. This only matters if you take water straight from the lake or a creek.

What to do for each, step by step

Lead

  • Drink and cook with cold water only
  • Run the tap before using it
  • Use a filter certified to for lead
  • Ask your supplier about your service line, then retest

Bacteria (coliform)

  • Boil water (a full rolling boil for 1 minute) or use bottled until it's fixed
  • On a well, fix the source first, then disinfect (shock) the well
  • Wait about a week for the chlorine to clear, then retest

Nitrates

  • Don't use it for baby formula
  • Don't boil it. Boiling makes nitrates stronger.
  • Call county health for next steps

PFAS

  • Compare to 4 ppt, the federal limit for public water systems for PFOA and PFOS
  • Use a filter certified to NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 for PFAS
  • Retest after the filter is in

Water 101 for Ithaca

No problem with your water? Good. Here's how it reaches you, what gets checked along the way, and the part that's up to you.

Is Ithaca's tap water safe to drink?

For most people, yes. The City of Ithaca, Cornell and Bolton Point all reported no violations in their latest water quality reports. That covers the water leaving the plant and a sample of homes. The two things nobody checks for you are the pipes inside your own home and private wells.

What's checked for you, and what isn't

Checked by your water system

  • Germs, and the disinfectant that kills them
  • Metals, nitrate, PFAS and other chemicals, on a state schedule
  • Lead and copper at a sample of homes every few years
  • Results published every year in a water quality report

Up to you

  • Your service line, pipes, solder and faucets, where lead gets in
  • A private well: no one tests it unless you do
  • Water heaters, unused taps and other spots where water sits
  • Filters: they only help if they're certified and changed on time

Read your yearly report in three numbers

  1. Violations. Look for "No" in the violation column. All three Ithaca systems had none in their latest reports.
  2. Lead, 90th percentile. 9 out of 10 homes tested were at or below this number. It should be under 15 ppb. Yours could still be higher if your pipes are old.
  3. Sodium. Only matters on a very low-sodium diet. All three systems are above 20 mg/L, the level EPA suggests for those diets.
